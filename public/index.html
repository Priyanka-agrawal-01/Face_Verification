How it works

Five steps turn a photo into a yes-or-no answer. The same steps power every tab.

  1. Detect

    An SSD MobileNet v1 detector scans the image and returns a box and a confidence score for every face. The largest face is treated as the subject.

  2. Find landmarks and align

    A second network places 68 points on the eyes, nose, mouth and jaw. They are used to crop the face consistently, so pose and position matter less.

  3. Embed

    A ResNet-style recognition network turns the aligned face into 128 numbers. It was trained so that photos of the same person produce nearby vectors and different people produce distant ones.

  4. Compare

    The Euclidean distance between two embeddings measures how alike the faces are. 0 means identical vectors. Values above about 1 are clearly different people.

  5. Decide

    A threshold turns the distance into a yes or no. Moving it trades false accepts against false rejects. The Evaluate tab picks it from data, and the LFW protocol tests it on held-out folds.

1:1 verification answers "are these the same person?". 1:N identification repeats the comparison against every enrolled embedding and takes the closest. Large face-search engines do the same at scale with an approximate nearest-neighbour index, which is why consent over whose faces go into that index matters.

Limitations and ethics

  • LFW is easy and dated. Mostly frontal, well-lit news photos of public figures. Expect lower accuracy on selfies, CCTV or across ages.
  • Errors differ across groups. LFW skews toward adult, male, lighter-skinned faces. Report this alongside any accuracy figure.
  • 1:N is harder than 1:1. A threshold that looks fine for pairs can still let strangers through when compared against many people.
  • Consent matters. The maths is simple; a large index of faces collected without consent is what makes it surveillance. Keep galleries small, consented and local.
